\section{The one-paragraph summary}
\term{Teraval} is a website (a ``dashboard'') that helps a company decide whether a very
expensive project is worth building. Our example company is \term{Barq AI}, and the project
is a big \term{data centre} --- a warehouse full of powerful computers (GPUs) --- in Abu
Dhabi that would rent out computing power for artificial intelligence. Building it would cost
about \textbf{AED 5.8 billion}. Teraval does all the money maths, shows it as easy charts,
lets you play with the numbers using sliders, adds some AI helpers, and finally says: ``Yes,
build it --- but carefully.''

% =====================================================================
\section{The real-world question we are answering}
A company called Barq AI has to decide: \emph{should we spend about AED 5.8 billion to build a
40~MW AI/GPU data-centre hall in Abu Dhabi and rent out its computing power for 8 years?}

Why this is a hard and important decision:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Huge amount of money.} AED 5.8 billion is a ``bet the company'' decision.
  \item \textbf{Prices keep changing.} The price to rent a GPU for one hour crashed from about
  \$8 to about \$2.85--3.50 recently. If the price stays low, the project can lose money.
  \item \textbf{Timing.} The UAE is trying to become a world hub for AI computing, and Abu
  Dhabi's electricity is cheap, which helps.
\end{itemize}

Everything in Teraval exists to answer this one question with numbers instead of gut feeling.

\section{The money words, explained like you are 15}
These are the finance ideas the dashboard uses. Each is one simple sentence.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \term{Cash flow} --- the money coming in or going out each year.
  \item \term{Discounting / Time value of money} --- money later is worth less than money now
  (AED 100 next year is worth less than AED 100 today), so future money is shrunk down to
  ``today's value.''
  \item \term{WACC (discount rate)} --- the yearly ``cost of money'' for the company; how much
  return investors expect. We use \textbf{9\%}. It is the bar the project must beat.
  \item \term{NPV (Net Present Value)} --- add up all future cash, shrunk to today's value,
  minus what you spend. \textbf{Positive NPV = the project makes you richer.} This is the main
  score.
  \item \term{IRR (Internal Rate of Return)} --- the yearly \% return the project earns. If it
  is higher than the 9\% WACC, that is good.
  \item \term{MIRR} --- a more careful version of IRR that fixes a known flaw in it.
  \item \term{PI (Profitability Index)} --- value created per AED spent. Above 1 is good.
  \item \term{Payback} --- how many years until you get your money back.
  \item \term{Break-even} --- the price (or usage level) at which the project exactly breaks
  even (NPV~=~0). Below it you lose money.
  \item \term{EAC (Equivalent Annual Cost)} --- turns a project's whole-life cost into one
  fair ``rent-like'' yearly number, so we can compare options that last different numbers of
  years.
\end{itemize}

\section{The technology we used, and why}
Think of the app as three layers.

\subsection*{Layer 1 --- the website you see (front-end)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \term{React 19 + TypeScript} --- the language/framework for building the interface.
  TypeScript catches mistakes before they happen.
  \item \term{Vite} --- the tool that builds and runs the site very fast.
  \item \term{TailwindCSS v4} --- how we style everything (the dark theme, glass cards).
  \item \term{Zustand} --- a small ``memory box'' that holds the current slider values so every
  chart updates together.
  \item \term{Framer Motion + GSAP} --- animations (the sliding tabs, the counting numbers).
  \item \term{Recharts} --- the 2D charts (bars, lines, the tornado).
  \item \term{Three.js + react-three-fiber + drei + postprocessing} --- the 3D data-centre hall
  with the glowing server racks.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Layer 2 --- the money brain (finance engine)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A pure \term{TypeScript} module (\texttt{web/src/finance/}) that does all the maths:
  NPV, IRR, MIRR, PI, payback, break-even, build-vs-rent, and a Monte-Carlo simulator.
  \item It is copied exactly into a \term{Python} file (\texttt{docs/finance-model-reference.py})
  that uses \texttt{numpy}. The two must always give the same numbers. This is our ``source of
  truth.''
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Layer 3 --- the AI chat helper (assistant back-end)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \term{FastAPI} (Python) --- a tiny web service.
  \item \term{NVIDIA NIM} --- the actual AI language model (it is ``OpenAI-compatible,'' so the
  same code style works). It only \emph{explains} the numbers; it never makes numbers up.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Tools that keep it correct, and where it lives online}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \term{Vitest} --- our automatic test suite (35 tests). \term{oxlint} --- a code-quality
  checker. \term{tsc} --- the TypeScript checker.
  \item \term{Playwright} --- headless browser we used to take screenshots and prove things work.
  \item \term{Vercel} hosts the website; \term{Render} hosts the AI back-end. The app is live.
  \item \term{LaTeX} (this document) for reports; \term{NotebookLM} to turn the report into slides.
\end{itemize}

\section{The dashboard, tab by tab}
The app has a top navigation bar with \textbf{8 tabs}, a floating \textbf{AI assistant} button
(bottom-right, on every tab), and a header (logo, a live ``model self-test'' badge, a
``Download summary'' button, and the current Accept/Reject verdict).

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \term{Overview.} The landing page. Shows a live \textbf{3D data-centre hall} (racks glow
  brighter when usage is higher; a heat plume changes colour with cooling efficiency; click a
  rack to inspect it), the six headline \textbf{KPI cards} (NPV, IRR, MIRR, PI, Payback,
  Break-even), and the \textbf{AI recommendation}.

  \item \term{Cash Flow.} A chart of money in/out for each of the 8 years. Year~0 is the big
  spend; Year~4 dips because we replace ageing GPUs (a ``refresh''); the line shows the running
  total that crosses zero at the discounted payback point ($\approx$6.3 years).

  \item \term{Build vs Rent.} Should Barq \emph{build and own} the hall, or \emph{rent} computing
  from a cloud giant? Because the two last different numbers of years, we compare them with
  \term{EAC}. Building is a fixed cost; renting grows with usage --- so \textbf{building only wins
  above about 77\% usage}. At the base case, building is slightly better (extra value of roughly
  +AED 320M).

  \item \term{Scenarios.} Three futures side by side:
  \emph{Optimistic} (+AED 10,216M, accept), \emph{Base} (+AED 1,854M, accept), and
  \emph{Pessimistic} ($-$AED 4,138M, reject). This shows the decision can flip.

  \item \term{Sensitivity \& Risk.} Six \textbf{sliders} (GPU price, usage, WACC, tariff, cooling,
  rack cost). Drag one and \emph{every} number in the app updates instantly. A \term{tornado chart}
  ranks which input matters most (answer: GPU price \#1, usage \#2). A \textbf{risk panel} lights
  up warnings (e.g.\ ``price below break-even''). It also shows \textbf{decision thresholds} ---
  the exact point at which the verdict flips.

  \item \term{AI Analysis.} The AI features: a \textbf{Monte-Carlo} simulator (see
  Section~\ref{sec:data}), an AI \textbf{rate forecast} with a range band, and an AI
  \textbf{scenario generator}.

  \item \term{Board Review.} Our newest feature. Five ``departments'' (Finance, Operations,
  Commercial, Sustainability, Technology) each score the project out of 100 using a fixed rule,
  take a stance (support / support-with-conditions / oppose), and a \textbf{weighted board verdict}
  combines them. At the base case: \textbf{APPROVE WITH CONDITIONS, 65/100.} Every score comes from
  the engine, so it all changes live with the sliders.

  \item \term{Ethics \& Audit.} The ethics of using AI (with our real safeguards), a
  \textbf{Data Provenance} panel (which numbers are official and where they came from), and an
  \textbf{Assumptions Audit} table that tags every input and runs a live ``model self-test.''
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{The floating AI Assistant} (on all tabs) answers questions in plain English. It is
\emph{grounded}: it is handed the live numbers and told to only explain them, never invent them.
If the AI server is off, it falls back to a built-in answer-er so it still works.

\textbf{Download summary} (header button) prints a clean one-page PDF of whatever is currently
on screen --- a live snapshot to share.

\section{The six AI features (Brief requires five; we have six)}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{F1 --- Rate forecaster.} Draws a likely future path for the GPU rental price with
  a ``P10--P90'' range (a low-to-high band), so we never pretend to know the exact future.
  \item \textbf{F2 --- Scenario generator.} Auto-builds an up/base/down set of assumptions from
  wherever the sliders are right now.
  \item \textbf{F3 --- Monte-Carlo risk simulator.} Runs 5,000 ``what-if'' worlds and reports the
  chance of losing money.
  \item \textbf{F4 --- AI Finance Assistant.} The chat helper (NVIDIA NIM), grounded in the engine.
  \item \textbf{F5 --- Anomaly / unrealistic-input detector.} Flags inputs outside believable
  ranges (e.g.\ cooling better than physically possible).
  \item \textbf{F6 --- Recommendation engine.} Writes the final accept/reject paragraph, including
  a ``stage-gate'' plan when the accept is thin.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Data, ``spread,'' and the train/test-split question}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection*{Is there a machine-learning train/test split? Short answer: no --- and here is why}
People often ask for a \term{train/test split} (splitting data to \emph{train} a model on some
and \emph{test} it on the rest). That belongs to \emph{machine-learning} projects that
\emph{learn} a model from examples.

\textbf{Teraval does not learn a model from data.} Its brain is a \emph{deterministic financial
calculator} --- given the same inputs, it always returns the same answer, using textbook formulas
(NPV, IRR, etc.). There is nothing to ``train,'' so there is \textbf{no train/test split}. Instead
of learning from a dataset, we \emph{feed in assumptions} and \emph{compute} the result. The
``AI'' parts are simulations and an explaining chatbot, not a trained predictor. Being honest
about this is important --- inventing a fake train/test split would be misleading.

\subsection*{So what plays the role of ``data''? Three things}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Real official data} (in the \texttt{datasets/} folder). We downloaded official
  figures to back our assumptions:
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{llll}
  \toprule
  \textbf{Figure} & \textbf{Value} & \textbf{Source} & \textbf{Status} \\
  \midrule
  CBUAE Base Rate & 3.65\% & Central Bank of the UAE, 29 Jul 2026 & official \\
  3-month EIBOR & 3.94\% & CBUAE EIBOR table, 31 Jul 2026 & official \\
  Abu Dhabi industrial tariff & AED 0.15/kWh & Abu Dhabi Open Data (ADWEA) & pending* \\
  \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \footnotesize *The tariff download was blocked by a portal outage (\texttt{data.bayanat.ae} was
  down); we kept the AED 0.15 benchmark and documented it. The two interest rates feed the WACC.
  \normalsize

  \item \textbf{The assumption set} --- the model's inputs (the six sliders plus structural
  numbers like 300 racks, 21,600 GPUs, 40~MW, 8-year life, 9\% tax). Each is tagged as Historical,
  Current, Forecast, User-entered, or AI-generated in the Assumptions Audit tab.

  \item \textbf{The Monte-Carlo distributions} --- this is what people usually mean by
  ``spread.'' Instead of one guess per input, we give each key input a \emph{range} shaped like a
  triangle (lowest, most-likely, highest), then draw 5,000 random combinations and compute NPV for
  each. See below.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{The ``spread'' of outcomes (two ways we show it)}
\textbf{(a) Monte-Carlo spread} --- from 5,000 simulated worlds at the base case:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Chance of losing money, P(NPV $<$ 0) & $\approx$ 21\% \\
Average NPV & +AED 1,456M \\
Low end (P10) & $-$AED 767M \\
High end (P90) & +AED 3,757M \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The distribution is drawn as a histogram (a hill shape); the left, red part is the ``loss'' zone.

\textbf{(b) Scenario spread} --- three deliberate futures:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
Scenario & NPV & IRR & Decision \\
\midrule
Optimistic & +AED 10,216M & 44.5\% & Accept \\
Base & +AED 1,854M & 16.5\% & Accept (conditional) \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{The Monte-Carlo is repeatable.} We use a fixed random ``seed,'' so the 21\% figure is the
same everywhere in the app and every time --- important for a report you can trust.

\section{How we keep the numbers trustworthy}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{35 automatic tests} (Vitest) check the maths against the verified figures. If a
  number ever drifts, a test fails.
  \item \textbf{Live self-test} in the app checks financial identities (e.g.\ discounting at the
  IRR must give NPV~=~0; PI~$>$~1 exactly when NPV~$>$~0). A badge in the header turns red if the
  model's logic ever breaks.
  \item \textbf{Two copies in sync} --- the TypeScript engine and the Python reference must match.
  \item \textbf{Grounding} --- the AI never invents numbers; it is handed the computed values and
  told to only explain them.
  \item \textbf{Type-checked and linted} --- \texttt{tsc} and \texttt{oxlint} both pass clean.
\end{itemize}

\section{Ethics (short version)}
The AI \emph{advises}; the human \emph{decides}. We show forecasts as ranges (never as certainty),
ground the chatbot so it cannot hallucinate figures, tag every assumption with its source, stress
the downside with the pessimistic case and Monte-Carlo, and state on every recommendation that the
final invest/reject call rests with the CFO --- not the AI.

% =====================================================================
\section{The final recommendation}
\textbf{Accept --- but as a ``stage-gated'' accept.} The base case creates about AED 1.85 billion
of value with a 16.5\% return, but the margin is thin (break-even \$3.34 is close to the \$4.00
price, and there is a $\approx$21\% chance of a loss). So the advice is: \emph{proceed, but release
the money in stages tied to real, measured usage staying above roughly 67--77\%, lock in multi-year
rental customers first, and keep a ``rent instead'' fallback.} The internal Board Review reaches the
same conclusion: \textbf{Approve With Conditions (65/100).}

\section{Probable questions (and short answers)}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt, style=nextline]
  \item[``Is this machine learning? Where is the train/test split?''] No. It is a deterministic
  finance calculator plus simulations, so there is no training and no split (see Section~\ref{sec:data}).
  \item[``Why 9\% WACC when the official rates suggest about 8.6\%?''] We kept 9\% on purpose --- a
  tougher bar --- so the ``accept'' verdict is conservative, not exaggerated.
  \item[``Which single number decides everything?''] The GPU rental price. A $-$20\% move flips NPV
  negative. Usage is the second-most important.
  \item[``What if electricity gets more expensive?''] Barely matters --- Abu Dhabi's cheap power is
  a real advantage; even a big tariff rise moves NPV far less than the GPU price does.
  \item[``How does the chatbot avoid making up numbers?''] It is ``grounded'': it receives the live
  computed figures and is instructed to explain only, never to generate numbers.
  \item[``What happens if the AI server is down?''] The assistant falls back to a built-in,
  engine-grounded answer-er, so the app still answers correctly.
  \item[``Is it actually live?''] Yes --- front-end on Vercel, AI back-end on Render.
  \item[``What is still pending / future work?''] (1) the official tariff file (portal was down);
  (2) an optional ``Custom Case'' feature (upload your own numbers) --- deferred as future work.
\end{description}

\section{Mini-glossary}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt, style=nextline, itemsep=1pt]
  \item[Data centre / hall] a building full of computers.
  \item[GPU] a powerful chip used for AI; here rented out by the hour.
  \item[PUE] ``Power Usage Effectiveness'' --- how much extra power cooling needs; 1.0 is perfect,
  1.2 is good.
  \item[Deterministic] same inputs always give the same output.
  \item[Monte-Carlo] running thousands of random ``what-if'' worlds to see the range of outcomes.
  \item[Seed] a fixed starting point for the randomness so results repeat exactly.
  \item[Grounded AI] an AI that can only use the real numbers it is given.
  \item[SSE / streaming] the chatbot's reply appears word-by-word as it is generated.
  \item[Deploy] to put the app online so anyone can use it.
\end{description}
